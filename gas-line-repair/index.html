Emergency Gas Line Repair in Morrisville, NC.

If you smell gas, or a gas line in your Morrisville, NC home has been hit or damaged, leave the building now and call Enbridge Gas at 1-877-776-2427 or 911 from outside. That call comes before any call to a plumber, including us.

Our dispatch line, (919) 944-7281, runs 24/7 for gas line repair and installation once the gas company or the fire crew says the building is safe. Until then, keep everyone outside, leave switches and flames alone, and do not go back in for belongings.

Smell gas right now? Leave the building first.

If you smell gas or hear a hiss at a gas line.

These four steps come before any plumbing call. Do them in this order.

Get everyone out.

Walk out with people and pets. Do not stop to open windows, and do not go back for phones, shoes or keys.

Call from a safe distance.

Dial Enbridge Gas at 1-877-776-2427, which the company lists as free and open 24 hours a day, or dial 911. Use a mobile phone outside or a neighbor's phone.

Touch nothing electrical or hot.

Do not flip a light switch, use a garage door opener, start a car in an attached garage or strike a match. One small spark can ignite a room full of gas.

Stay out until cleared.

Wait for the gas company or the fire crew to tell you it is safe. Call (919) 944-7281 afterward for the repair, or sooner if you want help planning it from the driveway.

Natural gas in Morrisville comes from Enbridge Gas, according to the Town of Morrisville's new resident page. Save 1-877-776-2427 in your phone today, because an emergency is a bad time to search for it.

A plumber comes second on purpose. Crews from the gas company and the fire department can make a building safe in ways we cannot. Once the danger is handled, we find the failed piping, repair it and test the system before anyone relights an appliance.

Signs that point to a gas problem.

Treat any of these as a gas call.

  • A rotten egg or sulfur smell indoors, in the garage or near the meter. The odor is added to natural gas so people notice a leak.
  • A hissing or whistling sound near a pipe, a connector or an appliance.
  • A meter dial that keeps moving while every gas appliance in the house is off.
  • Dead patches in the yard, or bubbles in a wet spot, along the path of a buried line.
  • Dizziness, headaches or a carbon monoxide alarm sounding. Leave and call 911, because that is a venting or combustion problem and not only a leak.
  • Burner flames that are yellow or orange instead of blue, or a pilot that keeps going out. These need an appliance check even with no smell.

How a gas line inspection and pressure test works.

An inspection starts with your eyes, your nose and a brush. We look over every visible joint, the flexible connector behind each appliance and the shutoff valve at each stop. A soap solution brushed on a fitting grows bubbles if gas is escaping, and a handheld combustible gas detector finds weak spots we cannot see.

A pressure test is the proof. The piping is capped off, filled with air or inert gas to a set pressure and watched on a gauge. A needle that holds steady tells us the system is tight, and a needle that sags sends us looking again, section by section.

Installing or extending a gas line.

New gas lines go in for ranges, dryers, outdoor grills, fire pits, fireplace logs and standby generators. A line is sized by the total load of everything on it and by the length of the run. Adding a grill to a line that already feeds a furnace and a water heater is a calculation and not a guess.

Swapping a tank water heater for a tankless unit is a common trigger. Tankless units often draw more gas than a tank model, so the old line may need a larger diameter. If that is your plan, read about the tankless water heater work we do and how a water heater installation is scoped before you pick a unit.

We choose the material for the run, such as threaded black iron or corrugated stainless tubing. We do not splice into pipe we cannot identify, and we do not reconnect an appliance until the piping has been tested.

Call 811 before anyone digs in Morrisville.

North Carolina's 811 service asks you to dial 811 or 1-800-632-4949 three working days before you plan to dig. Locating publicly managed underground lines is free, and the town's resident guide repeats the same number.

That matters for a gas run to a backyard grill, a new fence post, a mailbox or a pad for a generator. Request the locate first, wait for the marks, and show us where the trench will go so the new line stays clear of what the locators found.

Source: NC 811 homeowner guide

Permits and checking a contractor.

The Town of Morrisville Inspections Department, at 919-463-6190, issues permits for plumbing work. Its plumbing permit page says a permit is required to install, extend, relocate or replace a plumbing system. Whether your particular gas piping job counts is a question for that office, so call before work starts and do not rely on us or on a neighbor's memory.

You can also confirm that any contractor is licensed through the State Board of Examiners of Plumbing, Heating and Fire Sprinkler Contractors. Its public license search is open to homeowners.

What changes the price of gas line work.

A gas job costs more or less depending on a handful of things you can see before anyone quotes a number.

Length and route of the run.

A short drop in an open basement or garage is very different from a long run through finished walls or a trench across a yard.

Pipe material and size.

A bigger load needs a larger diameter, and the material changes both parts and labor.

Number of appliances.

Every added appliance raises the total load and may force a resize of the existing line.

Access and restoration.

Cutting drywall, crossing a slab or digging through landscaping adds labor and repair work.

Test results and permits.

Old piping that fails a pressure test needs repair before anything new connects, and permit questions can add steps.

Homeowners insurance often treats sudden damage differently from wear or slow leaks, so ask your insurer before assuming coverage. Repairs to the gas company's own equipment are handled by the gas company.
