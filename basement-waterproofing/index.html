Basement Waterproofing in Morrisville, NC.

Basement waterproofing in Morrisville, NC starts with a question many contractors skip, which is whether your house has a basement at all. Plumbers see damp basements, wet crawlspaces and soggy slab edges, and each needs a different fix. Call (919) 944-7281 to book a visit, with same-day appointments when available.

We look at where the water enters, whether a pipe leak is the real source and which drainage work would help. If the answer is a gutter, a grading fix or work outside our trade, we say so.

Is it groundwater or a plumbing leak?

A leaking pipe can look exactly like seepage. Cary's own guidance to customers is to lift the meter lid and see whether the indicator moves with every tap off, and that is the first test we run too. If it moves, the water may be coming from a supply line and not the ground.

A pipe leak keeps going in dry weather and often shows near the water heater, the washer box or a supply wall. Groundwater tracks the rain. Our leak detection work sorts the two out with a meter test and listening equipment before anyone digs.

Clues that point to groundwater instead of a pipe.

  • Water appears after rain and fades in a dry week.
  • Moisture shows along the joint where the floor meets the wall.
  • A white powdery crust, called efflorescence, forms on concrete or block.
  • Several spots wet at once, spread around the perimeter and not near a pipe.

Fix the outside first.

Most basement water starts as roof water. Gutters that overflow, downspouts that drop water at the wall and soil that slopes toward the house all push water against the foundation. A downspout extension that discharges several feet away, a clean gutter and a slope away from the wall cost far less than any interior system.

Some downspouts tie into buried drain lines, and those lines clog with leaves and roots. A line that cannot carry water backs it up against the foundation, which is where drain cleaning solves a problem that looks like waterproofing.

Interior drainage and the sump pit.

When outside fixes are not enough, an interior system gives water a place to go. A channel is cut along the floor edge, perforated pipe is laid in gravel and the pipe leads to a sump basin. A pump lifts the water out through a discharge line to the yard. Our guide to sump pump service explains the pump side.

This work is loud and dusty, and it opens your slab. It manages water that reaches the wall. It does not stop water from reaching the wall, which is why we ask you to fix gutters and grading first.

Cracks are symptoms, not the whole story.

A thin vertical crack that seeps after rain is often a candidate for injection with epoxy or polyurethane. A horizontal crack, a stair-step crack in block or a wall that bows inward may point to structural movement, and that is a job for a structural engineer.

We do not repair foundations. If a crack looks structural, we will tell you to call an engineer before spending on sealing.

Clay soil and why moisture can come back.

The North Carolina Geological Survey says expansive shrink-swell clay soils occur in the state, with elevated occurrence in the Triassic basins and the Carolina terrane, and that a small amount of soil expansion can damage a foundation. The survey does not say what is under any one lot.

In practice, clay swells when wet and shrinks when dry, so cracks can open and close with the seasons. Photograph a crack with a ruler beside it and date the picture. If the crack grows, sticking doors appear or gaps open at trim, show that record to an engineer before treating the symptom alone.

Source: NC Geological Survey on expansive soils

Crawlspace and slab homes.

In a crawlspace, take a flashlight and look for standing water, dark staining on the soil, wet insulation, a torn vapor barrier and rust on pipe hangers. Wear a mask and gloves, and do not enter a space you cannot crawl back out of. Photos from the hatch are enough for us to start.

On a slab, a damp spot that feels warm can be a hot water line leaking beneath the concrete. That is plumbing, not waterproofing, and the right step is slab leak repair rather than sealing the floor.

Standards for the drainage side of waterproofing.

Interior drains and sump pits are plumbing under the 2024 North Carolina Plumbing Code, based on the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC). Structural and soil questions belong to an engineer, and we do not give structural advice.

Ask which standard each contractor's membrane or coating is rated to.

What changes the price of waterproofing work.

No two wet houses are the same, so the price follows the cause and the access.

The cause of the water.

A clogged downspout line costs far less than a perimeter drain.

Length of drainage.

More linear feet of channel means more cutting, gravel and labor.

Sump basin and pump.

A new basin and pump are separate items, and a backup adds more.

Access and finishes.

Finished walls, stairs and stored belongings slow the work.

Exterior excavation.

Digging along a foundation adds labor and landscape repair.

Insurance often excludes groundwater seepage but may respond to a sudden burst pipe, so ask your insurer how your policy treats each.
