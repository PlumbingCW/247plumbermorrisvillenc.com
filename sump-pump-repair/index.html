How a sump pump works and why it fails.

A sump pump sits in a pit, called a basin, dug below the floor level where water collects. When rising water lifts a float switch, the pump starts and pushes water up a discharge pipe, usually 1 1/2 or 2 inches wide, through a check valve and outside. The check valve keeps pumped water from sliding back into the pit.

Most failures are mechanical. A float jams against the basin wall, a switch burns out, grit seizes the impeller or a check valve fails and the pump cycles again and again. A pump that runs nonstop without lowering the water may be undersized, or its discharge may be blocked by ice or debris.

Does a sump pit even apply to your house?

We cannot tell from town records what kind of foundation your house has, and in this area they differ. Some homes have a full basement, some have a crawlspace and some sit on a slab. A pump only helps where there is a pit, and a pit usually shows up in a basement. Crawlspace pits exist too, so ask your seller or look for a lidded basin in the floor.

If you have no pit and you see water, the fix may be a downspout extension, a grading change or a drain and pit installed for you. For a crawlspace, use a flashlight to look for standing water, dark soil or a wet vapor barrier. Our basement waterproofing page covers the drainage side in more depth.

Sewage ejector pumps are a different machine.

A sewage ejector lifts waste from a below-grade bathroom, laundry or kitchen up to the main drain line. It sits in a sealed, vented basin and handles solids, which a sump pump cannot. Sewage must never be sent to the yard or into a sump discharge.

If an ejector alarm sounds, stop using the fixtures on that system and call. When the whole main line is slow and not just the basement fixtures, the cause is often upstream, and sewer line cleaning clears it.

Backup power and testing before storm season.

A pump needs electricity, and storms are when the power goes out. A battery backup pump sits beside the main pump and takes over when the main one stops, but it runs only as long as its battery and the inflow allow. It is a bridge until power returns, not a second house.

You can test your own pump. Pour a bucket of water into the pit, watch the float lift, hear the pump start and confirm water leaves through the discharge. If it cycles when the weather is dry, a meter check and leak detection can tell whether a supply leak is feeding the pit.

Where code and common sense meet on sump discharge.

Installing or replacing a pump is plumbing work under the 2024 North Carolina Plumbing Code, based on the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC). We do not state a rule about where a pump may discharge, because that depends on your lot and on local drainage rules.

Ask the Town of Morrisville Inspections Department at 919-463-6190 where discharge is allowed before you change a pump's outlet.

What changes the price of sump pump work.

The cost of a pump job depends on what is wrong and how hard it is to reach.

Pump type and power.

Pedestal and submersible pumps differ, and a larger motor costs more.

Basin condition.

A cracked or undersized basin may need replacing along with the pump.

Discharge piping.

Length, routing and the check valve change parts and labor.

Backup power.

Adding a battery backup pump is a separate item from the main pump.

Access and finishes.

A pit under stairs or behind finished walls takes longer than one in an open utility area.

Many homeowners policies exclude groundwater and sewer backup unless an endorsement is added, so ask your insurer before a loss and not after.
